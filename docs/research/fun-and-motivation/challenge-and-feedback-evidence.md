# Orbital Ante: challenge, learning, feedback, and healthy engagement

Research synthesis prepared 13 November 2026. These are findings from published studies and proposed applications, not observations from an Orbital Ante playtest. Unity and Steam Deck behavior remain unverified. Fable 5.2 remains responsible for creative direction, difficulty tuning, pacing, and assets; the implementation team can supply clear state, reliable calculations, diagnostics, and reversible comparison builds.

The useful question is whether players understand an interesting problem, can act on it, and learn something worth trying next time. Neither maximum playtime nor maximum success is an adequate definition of fun. The evidence below supports small experiments around that question; it does not establish a psychological formula for a successful roguelite.

| **Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020), “The Power of Feedback Revisited: A Meta-Analysis of Educational Feedback Research.”** *Frontiers in Psychology*, 10, 3087. Information content mattered; learning outcomes and motivation did not respond identically. | Educational tasks are not entertainment. Most underlying research was old; designs differed and pre/post estimates were larger than controlled comparisons. | **Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010), “Can There Ever Be Too Many Options? A Meta-Analytic Review of Choice Overload.”** *Journal of Consumer Research*, 37, 409–425. Some contexts showed overload and others benefits from additional choice. | Mostly consumer decisions, not tactical action under pressure. A null average does not mean complexity never matters; this review did not establish sufficient conditions for overload. **Transfer:** do not justify an arbitrary three-choice limit using psychology. | **Vuorre, Johannes, Magnusson & Przybylski (2022), “Time spent playing video games is unlikely to impact well-being.”** *Royal Society Open Science*, 9, 220411. Causal interpretation requires assumptions; selection, attrition, two-week measurement intervals, and participating games constrain it. The study was not preregistered. **Transfer:** longer sessions alone demonstrate neither better experience nor harm. Measure enjoyment and felt pressure separately; do not claim that this study proves any design safe or therapeutic. |

## Practical interpretation for the implementation team

**Make the challenge diagnosable before increasing it.** A failed landing, lost wager, missed shot, or death should leave the player able to distinguish their decision from a hidden rule or technical inconsistency. A recoverable mistake can be satisfying when it suggests a different tactic. That is a design hypothesis combining the evidence, not an experimentally demonstrated feature of this game. A first pass should preserve Fable’s intended mechanics while ensuring displayed costs and consequences agree with actual calculations, including boundary cases.

**Treat competence as improving a useful model of the game.** “I understand why that happened and have a different approach” is a more actionable playtest signal than “the number increased.” Ask what players learned before presenting a proposed explanation. Otherwise a helpful-looking recap may hide a failure of the moment-to-moment feedback. Where mechanics are uncertain by design, explain the rule or range without revealing outcomes, secrets, or an optimal strategy.

**Distinguish desirable decisions from avoidable mental work.** Comparing a dangerous route with a safer route may be the game. Reconstructing inconsistent terminology, remembering an undisclosed conversion, or interpreting contradictory numbers usually supplies little additional strategy. Keep units, timing, and preview/commit behavior consistent. Defer option count, language style, layout, and information hierarchy to Fable; these materially affect the creative experience and deserve comparison on the Deck screen.

**Protect response quality.** Technical feedback should follow the actual state transition. Successful shooting, damage, pickup, and a rejected action need distinct consequences that do not obscure the next decision. More animation can itself delay or mask information. Measure responsiveness with the target build and device; source inspection cannot establish input latency, perceived feel, frame pacing, or legibility.

**Do not turn uncertainty into a behavioral trap.** Genuine risk can come from a player's chosen commitment and a legible system. Manufactured near misses and undisclosed changes to odds undermine that premise. Avoid streak obligations, loss-framed return prompts, timed scarcity, and escalating commitments introduced merely to increase playtime. These are project values and test constraints, not claims that one study establishes a universal harm threshold.

## Smallest useful playtest proposal

Start with **one feedback or consequence-preview change**, selected with Fable. Keep damage, economy, enemy composition, reward magnitude, seed policy, and progression unchanged. This isolates the implementation question: can the player make and interpret the same decision more reliably? A scoring preview, if appropriate to the existing system, must show the same rounding and eligibility rules used on commit. It must not imply that uncertain future rewards are guaranteed.

Recruit **8–12 people for a formative round**, including players unfamiliar with roguelites and experienced players. This is a pragmatic usability sample, not a sample size supported by a power calculation. Use the target Deck configuration where available. Give equal practice, then compare original and candidate in two short, comparable encounters, counterbalancing order. Repeating the identical encounter teaches its answer, so use matched encounters and record the mismatch as a limitation. Participants can stop without explaining why.

Before committing to the relevant decision, ask the participant to state their expected cost, consequence, and uncertainty. After the consequence, ask what caused it and what they would try next. Record the answer verbatim before offering hints. Use a simple prewritten rubric: correct, partly correct, or incorrect; note whether the error arose from an undisclosed rule, a calculation mismatch, unclear presentation, or intentional uncertainty. Do not silently reinterpret answers to favor the new build.

After each encounter, collect four separate 1–7 ratings: enjoyment, perceived control, perceived challenge, and pressure to continue. Add “What was most frustrating?” and “What would you choose to do next?” These are diagnostic questions, not a validated flow scale. Record survival and task success as context. Also capture bugs, frame-time spikes, unreadable states, input failures, and motion discomfort. Do not combine all these into a single engagement score.

A promising result is fewer incorrect expectations and repeated rule misunderstandings, with useful next-attempt explanations and no recurring decrease in enjoyment or agency. Do not treat 8–12 observations as proof of efficacy or average away a severe subgroup problem. Report counts and individual patterns alongside limitations. Ask Fable to assess whether any clarity gain weakens tension or discovery. If the change mostly increases session time, fails to improve understanding, or induces unwanted pressure, revise or remove it.

Only after this should Fable select a difficulty or pacing hypothesis for a second experiment. Test one dimension at a time, state the intended experience first, and choose success criteria before seeing the data. A consistent decision record is more useful than claiming that the game has been scientifically optimized.
