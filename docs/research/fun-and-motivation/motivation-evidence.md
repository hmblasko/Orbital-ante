# Motivation research for Orbital Ante

Research date: 13 November 2026. Targeted evidence review for Phase 1 refinement; not an exhaustive systematic review. Fable 5.2 retains creative authority over game design, feel and tuning. These papers motivate hypotheses for playtesting; none establishes what will be fun in this game. No Unity execution or player study was performed.

## Decision supported by this evidence

Prioritize a player understanding what an action will do, choosing an approach, seeing its consequences, and improving on the next attempt. This supports the existing constitution's agency, learnable challenge, meaningful discovery, legible risk and cognitive-load rules. A good initial change should repair a specific breakdown in navigation or combat understanding, without introducing a progression economy, completion meter or new content system.

Distinguish three outcomes throughout: **performance** (doing more/better), **enjoyment** (liking the experience), and **continued play** (choosing to return). They can diverge. Increased actions or session duration cannot alone establish increased enjoyment. “Dopamine” is not an implementation specification, and the neuroscience below does not justify addiction claims or retention tricks.

## Seven sources, with evidence and limits

### 1. Need satisfaction in actual video games

Richard M. Ryan, C. Scott Rigby and Andrew Przybylski (2006), *The Motivational Pull of Video Games: A Self-Determination Theory Approach*. DOI: [10.1007/s11031-006-9051-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-006-9051-8). [Author-hosted full text](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2006_RyanRigbyPrzybylski_MandE.pdf).

Four studies: laboratory samples of 89, 50 and 58 undergraduates playing one, two and four games, plus a survey of 730 MMO-community members. Perceived competence and autonomy were associated with enjoyment and preferences for future play. Intuitive controls also related to those needs. Relatedness independently predicted enjoyment and future-play preferences in the MMO survey.

The laboratory studies assigned games/order; they did **not** isolate randomized manipulation of each psychological need. Need–enjoyment relationships remain associations. Small convenience samples, short sessions and a largely male online sample limit transfer. Future-play preference is not long-term observed retention. Relatedness here concerns other players; it does not establish that fictional characters or a ship provide the same effect.

**Game hypothesis:** clearer interaction and flight feedback should support competence while preserving the player's control over approach. Ask players what they intended, what happened, and what they would change; do not infer competence from success alone. This paper supplies no reason to add multiplayer.

### 2. Choice can help; quantity is not the mechanism

Erika A. Patall, Harris Cooper and Jorgianne Civey Robinson (2008), *The Effects of Choice on Intrinsic Motivation and Related Outcomes: A Meta-Analysis of Research Findings*. DOI: [10.1037/0033-2909.134.2.270](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.134.2.270). [Full text](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/2008_PatallCooperRobinson_PsychBulletin.pdf).

Meta-analysis of 41 studies across child and adult samples and several settings. Choice improved intrinsic motivation, perceived competence, effort and performance on average. Effects varied with the kind of choice, comparison condition, setting, age and reward context. Denying choice explicitly produced a different comparison from assigning an option neutrally.

Experimental choice manipulations support causal conclusions within those tasks, but pooled moderators are not randomized comparisons between every moderator category. Much evidence comes from laboratory/education tasks. The review does not establish a universal optimal menu size, that more choices always help, or that an arbitrary cosmetic choice equals a meaningful tactical choice.

**Game hypothesis:** preserve two understandable, viable responses to an existing situation rather than adding more buttons. A navigation aid should explain the current state without steering automatically. Playtest whether players can explain their chosen approach and an alternative; presenting alternatives alone is not proof of experienced agency.

### 3. Rewards can change why someone acts

Edward L. Deci, Richard Koestner and Richard M. Ryan (1999), *A Meta-Analytic Review of Experiments Examining the Effects of Extrinsic Rewards on Intrinsic Motivation*. DOI: [10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627). [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/); [full text](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/1999_DeciKoestnerRyan_Meta.pdf).

A meta-analysis of 128 experimental studies. Expected tangible rewards contingent on engaging, completing or performing reduced subsequent free-choice intrinsic motivation on average (reported standardized effects −0.40, −0.36 and −0.28 respectively). Positive feedback increased free-choice behavior and reported interest on average.

These are controlled comparisons across specific tasks and reward arrangements, often initially interesting activities. Measurement matters: self-reported interest and optional activity after reward removal are different endpoints. The paper and its interpretation were debated; it does not show that all rewards, all feedback, or all in-game points harm motivation. An external payment is not interchangeable with diegetic combat feedback or a game's constitutive scoring rule.

**Game hypothesis:** prefer useful information about an action's effect over evaluative pressure or payment for repetition. A readable hit/result cue is defensible; adding a reward economy to “make combat fun” is unsupported. Keep Phase 2 points/ante decisions with Fable and the user.

### 4. More output can occur without more enjoyment

Elisa D. Mekler, Florian Brühlmann, Alexandre N. Tuch and Klaus Opwis (2017), *Towards Understanding the Effects of Individual Gamification Elements on Intrinsic Motivation and Performance*. DOI: [10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.048](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.048). [University record](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/towards-understanding-the-effects-of-individual-gamification-elem/); [full text](https://glassmanlab.seas.harvard.edu/annotated_works/mekler17.pdf).

Online experiment with 273 participants, comparing points, levels, leaderboard and plain conditions on an image-annotation task, with measured causality orientation. Game elements increased tag quantity, particularly levels/leaderboard, but did not significantly improve reported intrinsic motivation, autonomy, competence or tag quality.

This helps separate behavioral output from motivational experience. Nonsignificance does not prove zero effect. Short exposure, a non-game task, self-report motivation and the particular implementations constrain transfer; causality orientation was measured, not randomly assigned. It cannot settle whether scoring belongs in a roguelite.

**Game hypothesis:** evaluate a combat/navigation change using enjoyment, comprehension and perceived control alongside behavior. More shots fired, faster completion or more retries may reflect confusion, pressure or easier tuning. Do not accept those metrics alone as evidence that a feature made Orbital Ante more fun.

### 5. Curiosity needs a recognizable question

George Loewenstein (1994), *The Psychology of Curiosity: A Review and Reinterpretation*. DOI: [10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75). [Author-hosted full text](https://www.contrib.andrew.cmu.edu/~gl20/GeorgeLoewenstein/Papers_files/pdf/PsychofCuriosity.pdf).

A theoretical/narrative review, not a systematic meta-analysis or a single experiment with a pooled sample. Its information-gap account proposes that curiosity arises when people perceive a gap in knowledge or understanding. It also discusses curiosity's transience and disappointment when an answer is unsatisfying.

Treat this as a useful explanatory framework rather than a measured game-design effect. It does not show that every mystery improves enjoyment, that withholding required rules is beneficial, or that indefinite unresolved questions retain players healthily.

**Game hypothesis:** existing exploration should let a player identify a concrete question and an action that can answer it. Fable could shape recognizable environmental clues; Codex can make existing navigation/status information legible. Teach the operational rule needed to act; reserve uncertainty for optional discovery. In a test, ask what the player is curious about and whether discovery answered it meaningfully.

### 6. Curiosity can accompany better learning

Matthias J. Gruber, Bernard D. Gelman and Charan Ranganath (2014), *States of Curiosity Modulate Hippocampus-Dependent Learning via the Dopaminergic Circuit*. DOI: [10.1016/j.neuron.2014.08.060](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.08.060). [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/).

Two laboratory studies paired participant-rated trivia curiosity with later trivia recall and recognition of incidental faces; one used fMRI. Main-text analyses involve 19/28 participants for face recognition and 18/27 for trivia recall. High-curiosity trivia showed better recall both shortly afterward and after one day. The delayed incidental-memory advantage occurred for confident recognition, not overall recognition.

Curiosity was classified using individual ratings rather than randomly assigned independently of item interest. Small samples, directed tests and trivia/face tasks limit generalization. fMRI associations are not direct measurement of dopamine release; the authors explicitly limit that inference. This research measures memory, not game enjoyment or compulsive engagement.

**Game hypothesis:** introduce an understandable problem before providing contextual guidance, then let the player act on the answer. Test whether they later apply the rule unaided. Do not manufacture anticipation delays or interpret stronger curiosity as permission to hide basic controls.

### 7. Progress information helps goal attainment, not necessarily fun

Benjamin Harkin, Thomas L. Webb, Betty P. I. Chang, Andrew Prestwich, Mark Conner, Ian Kellar, Yael Benn and Paschal Sheeran (2016), *Does Monitoring Goal Progress Promote Goal Attainment? A Meta-Analysis of the Experimental Evidence*. DOI: [10.1037/bul0000025](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/); [accepted manuscript](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/87431/1/bul%20harkin%20raw%20final.pdf).

Meta-analysis of 138 studies, total N=19,951, randomly assigning monitoring interventions versus controls. Interventions increased monitoring and improved goal attainment on average (d=0.40, 95% CI 0.32–0.48).

This supports a causal average effect of interventions in studied settings, not a causal claim about any specific HUD. Goal attainment is not enjoyment; studies span predominantly non-game goals. Aggregate mediation does not prove the entire mechanism for an individual player. Public reporting/recording moderators are not a mandate for social pressure.

**Game hypothesis:** show the actionable state relevant to the player's immediate goal, such as motion relative to a selected destination, rather than a global completion percentage. Test whether players predict the consequence of braking or movement better, then separately ask whether the information helped or distracted.

## Smallest useful evaluation

Fable chooses the experience and presentation; implementation should state one failure, one hypothesis and the relevant constitution principles. Compare the current behavior with one narrowly scoped revision, counterbalancing order where practical. Start with a few observed sessions to find comprehension failures; this is diagnostic, not a statistically conclusive experiment.

Before each attempt, ask the player to explain their intended action. Afterward, ask what caused the outcome, what they would do differently and whether the attempt felt satisfying and under their control. Observe repeated errors, unprompted successful application and unwanted distraction. Offer an optional further attempt without reward or pressure, while recognizing this remains only a short-session measure.

Acceptance should require correct understanding plus preserved choice and no evident distraction; a performance improvement alone is insufficient. Record failures and disagreements as well as successes. Deck readability, feel, frame cost, sustained enjoyment and accessibility remain unverified until actual Unity/Steam Deck playtesting. Research guides that test; it cannot replace it.
